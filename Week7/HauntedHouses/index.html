Element Chart

There are six categories of elements in a haunted house: location, core, concealment, visual add-on, audio add-on, and optional strategy. Together they make up three parts:
Locations — rooms and corridors, chained into a route.
Encounters — a core, a concealment, and optional visual and audio add-ons, combined to create fear.
Optional strategies — ways to amplify fear. A strategy can be a kind of encounter (a Visual Block, or a Visual Cue built from a core, a concealment, and a visual add-on) or a change to a location (a Direction Change).
The chart below lists every element by category. Hover over one for details.

Intent

A haunted house looks like a pile of spooky props, but it behaves like a system: locations are chained into a route, encounters are placed along it, and their sequence steers a group’s movement, attention and emotion. I wanted to model the designer’s side of this: you assemble a house, send a group of visitors through it, and watch how the pieces affect each other and the visitors.

Process

  1. Element catalogue. From my Element Chart, I defined every element: locations (Room with 4 floor slots + 2 slots behind each wall; Corridor, straight or turning, with 2 wall slots); cores (Actor, Installation, Stationary); concealments (Door, Shutter, Screen, Display); visual add-ons (Darkness, Flashing Light); audio add-on (Sudden Sound); optional strategies (Visual Block, Visual Cue, Direction Change).
  2. Rules as constraints. Wall slots are in a hidden staff area, so they must use a concealment (Door, Shutter, Screen or Display); floor slots have no concealment or use a free-standing Screen or Display. A floor slot and the wall slots next to it rule each other out. A Visual Cue is a core with a Display or no concealment, plus an optional Flashing Light, so the editor only offers those options for it. A Direction Change is not a slot: a room can exit through its left or right wall, or a corridor can turn left or right. Locations may not overlap, and a new location may not cover the hidden area of a wall slot in use. Default lighting and sound are implied everywhere.
  3. UML. The structure became classes: a House is composed of Locations (Room and Corridor extend Location) and the doorway slots between them; each Location owns its floor and wall slots. Every slot holds an Encounter, whose element categories (core, concealment, visual add-on, audio add-on) are attributes, each limited to a fixed set of values. The optional strategies are kinds of Encounter (Visual Block, which goes in a doorway slot, and Visual Cue) or the shape of a Location (Direction Change). Visitors are separate agents that react to encounters and spread fear to each other.
  4. Behaviour. I gave encounters no fixed “scare value”. They only emit stimuli; the visitor’s state decides the result, which is where emergence comes from.
  5. Emergent properties. The same encounter can be a great scare or a wasted prop depending on its neighbours. A Visual Cue makes the scare that follows hit harder (misdirection), and Visual Blocks and Direction Changes raise anticipation for every later scare. Scares just behind a Visual Block or just after a turning corridor are unseen until the visitor reaches them, so they hit harder. Repeating the same core (habituation) or packing scares too tightly (numbness) weakens them. The player also sets the Visitor Flow (Lv 1–6): the higher it is, the closer together visitors walk, and anticipation slows a visitor down so the ones behind catch up. Fear spreads between visitors walking close together. A scare that has just gone off needs a moment to reset, so a visitor right behind can’t set it off again; instead, everyone near it gets a smaller fright, bigger the closer they are, and a visitor frightened by the same scare again feels it less. A Visual Cue still catches every visitor’s eye. A visitor who panics runs for an exit, so every later encounter is wasted on them.
