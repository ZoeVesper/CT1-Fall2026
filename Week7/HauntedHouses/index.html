Element chart

The system is built around six categories: location, core, concealment, visual add-on, audio add-on, and optional strategy. Within the optional strategy category, visual cues are created by combining elements from three other categories: the core, concealment, and visual add-on. Hover over an element for details.

Intent

A haunted house looks like a pile of spooky props, but it behaves like a system: a handful of element categories (location, core, concealment, visual add-on, audio add-on, optional strategy) are combined into encounters, and the encounters are sequenced to steer a group’s movement, attention and emotion. I wanted to model the designer’s side of this: you assemble a house, send a group of visitors through it, and watch how the pieces affect each other and the visitors.

Process

  1. Element catalogue. From my category chart I defined every legal element: Room (4 floor slots + 2 slots behind each wall), Corridor (one slot behind each side wall) and Corner (a corridor turn, 2 wall slots); cores Actor, Installation, Stationary; concealments Door, Screen, Shutter, Display; add-ons Darkness, Flashing Light, Sudden Sound; strategies Visual Block, Visual Cue, Direction Change.
  2. Rules as constraints. A Visual Cue is a core + (Display or no concealment) + optional Flashing Light, so the editor only offers those options for it. Wall slots are in a hidden staff area, so they must use an opening (Door, Screen, Shutter or Display); floor slots are open or behind a free-standing Screen or Display. A floor slot and the wall slots next to it rule each other out. A Direction Change is not a slot: a room can exit through its left or right wall, and a corridor turns with a Corner piece. Locations may not overlap, and a new location may not cover the hidden area of a wall slot in use. Default lighting and sound are implied everywhere.
  3. UML. The structure became classes: a House is composed of Locations (Room, Corridor and Corner extend an abstract Location), and each Location of Encounter slots. The element categories (core, concealment, visual add-on, sound) are attributes of an Encounter, each limited to a fixed set of values; the strategies are kinds of Encounter (visual block, visual cue) or the shape of a Location (direction change). Visitors are separate agents that react to encounters.
  4. Behaviour. I gave encounters no fixed “scare value”. They only emit stimuli; the visitor’s state decides the result, which is where emergence comes from.

Emergent properties

No encounter has a built-in “scariness”. The same encounter can be a great scare or a wasted prop depending on its neighbours:

  • Misdirection. A visitor watching a Visual Cue is far more vulnerable to a jump scare that follows.
  • Anticipation. Visual Blocks and Direction Changes raise anticipation, which multiplies later scares. A curtain also hides what is just behind it: scares at the start of the location behind a Visual Block hit 1.2× harder.
  • Blind side. Visitors cannot see into the location after a corner until they have turned into it, so scares at its start hit harder. A room turns in its centre, far from the next location, so its side exit gives no such bonus; nor does a straight doorway, or a scare deeper inside.
  • Space trade-offs. A floor scare and the wall scares next to it rule each other out, and a tight layout covers hidden areas, so the designer trades open-floor scares against hidden ones.
  • Habituation. Repeating the same core type makes it less effective for that visitor.
  • Numbness. Scares packed too tightly dull the visitor’s response.
  • Contagion and panic. Fear spreads between nearby visitors; a visitor whose fear reaches 325 panics and runs for the nearest emergency exit, so every later encounter is wasted on them.

Try: ten jump scares in a row versus a house that mixes cues, visual blocks and direction changes, and compare the fear table.
