Seminar readings

From https://programmingdesignsystems.com/

Quote 1: Furthermore, digital products often have temporal logic where a linear narrative is replaced by a set of complex states and transitions. All in all, digital products all share a common trait: They are created with programming languages.

This reminds me of the Week 1 assignment. I had trouble creating images with just HTML because the logic is different. It might be difficult for people with previous visual design training to translate an image in their head into code or a programming language due to this difference.

Quote 2: However, there is a century-long bond between the field of design and new advances in technology, and if graphic designers do not become fluent in this new digital reality, they will become irrelevant.

It's not just graphic design; other areas of design are also changing rapidly. Technology updates nowadays are truly overwhelming. If you don't keep up with all the new applications, plugins, and developments, you risk getting left behind.

Quote 3: Most written languages have a natural reading direction. In a majority of the Western world, characters are arranged in horizontal lines, and we read from left to right, top to bottom.

It is interesting that Japanese has two reading directions—horizontal (left-to-right) and vertical (right-to-left). Its affect on design probably is significant.

From https://survivinghci.com/content/readings/what-do-prototypes-prototype/source.pdf

Quote 1: Prototypes are widely recognized to be a core means of exploring and expressing designs for interactive computer artifacts.

If a design is not related to interaction or computers, can there still be prototypes? Or, should we say that the outputs of the design process are not classified as prototypes?

Quote 2: Is a brick a prototype? The answer depends on how it is used. If it is used to represent the weight and scale of some future artifact, then it certainly is: it prototypes the weight and scale of the artifact.

The article defines an artifact broadly enough that everything could be a prototype, as long as it relates to an interactive system. But how do we define interactive system?

Quote 3: However, Ehn and Kyng (1991) have shown that even prototypes made of cardboard are very useful for user testing. In the authors' experience, no one tool supports iterative design work in all of the important areas of investigation. To design well, designers must be willing to use different tools for different prototyping tasks; and to team up with other people with complementary skills. Finished-looking (or-behaving) prototypes

This reminds me of Nintendo Labo. It also kind of highlight the importance of keeping up with new technology.
